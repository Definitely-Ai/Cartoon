# The Master Prompt

<!-- BRAND: "The Swinging Door" is the series/bar name throughout this document -->

Purpose: the one page an AI reads before drawing. The BASE block below is assembled
from the whole canon — now governed by `canon/HARRINGTON-VISION.md` and the plates
in `canon/vision/` — and is pasted **verbatim** into every image request; only the
slots change per cartoon (away games additionally swap the setting passage — see
below). Do not paraphrase the base — paraphrase is how the strip drifts. When two
documents disagree, the Harrington vision wins; below it, the authority ladder in
`canon/README.md` decides; never average.

## How to use

1. Paste the BASE block exactly as written (the fenced block only — nothing outside the fence).
2. Fill the slots: `[SCENE]`, `[TV]`, `[BOARD]`. Slot language obeys each character's vocabulary: Mango and Abby have **hands**; Drew has **feathered hands** (hand-shaped, feather-covered — never bare human skin).
3. Give the image model the reference plates for the style and every character in the scene: the four Harrington plates in `canon/vision/` are the standing style-and-character reference. Over the studio connector, fetch them with `get_model_sheet`; working directly in the repo, attach the files.
4. The caption is **never rendered by the image model**. The house typesets it beneath the finished art as attributed italic dialogue — `Drew: "…"` / `Mango: "…"` / `Abby: "…"` — via `file_cartoon` over the connector or `npm run dialogue` in the repo. In-image signage, by contrast, IS the image model's job (see the text rules inside the BASE block).
5. **Look at every generated image before filing it.** Run the checklist below against the actual pixels. Any failure is a redraw with the fault named in the prompt — never a filing.

## BASE block

```text
A single-panel gag cartoon drawn like an antique steel engraving: fine pen crosshatching and stippling on every surface — wood grain, knit stitches, fur and feathers laid in individual strokes, glass catching light — with a full tonal range from deep rich blacks through silvery greys to bright paper highlights. Confident contour lines over the hatching. The panel is composed full, corner to corner, and rewards a second look. Black-and-white only; no flat fills, no cel shading, no photographic rendering, no color.

THE ROOM. The scene is the interior of The Swinging Door, an upscale, current ground-floor bar a block off Wall Street — never a dive: a polished MARBLE bar top, fine walnut-paneled walls, warm brass and good glassware, and handsome furniture — handsome leather armchairs and fine stools. When the front window is in frame it carries the bar's name, mirrored as read from inside; a framed house sign may hang on the wall, handsome but not obligatory in every panel. The house keeps a proud back bar — each bottle a distinct shape with its own golf-pun label (BIRDIE BOURBON, 19TH HOLE RYE, PAR-TEE SCOTCH, DIVOT DRIVE GIN — that register), the SAME collection every day, the lineup never changing. But that shelving stands on the SERVICE side, behind the reader, and it is NOT drawn behind the gentlemen (see THE STAGE); a bottle reaches the picture only in Abby's hand or standing on the marble. THE ONLY CHARACTERS IN THE ROOM ARE THIS CARTOON'S CAST. There are NO other figures anywhere in the bar: no human customers, no human bartender, no human staff, no people at the tables, none in the mirror, none blurred in the background — the room behind the cast is furniture, bottles, panelling and signage only. Abby is the ONLY bartender and the only other character who ever appears. The single exception in the whole strip is the TELEVISION SCREEN, where human beings may appear because it is broadcast footage. This covers pictures as well as people: the framed prints on the panelling and any emblem embroidered or printed on clothing are NON-FIGURATIVE — architecture, landscape, animals, still life, a ship, a bull, a bottle. No human figure appears anywhere in the room, inside a picture frame or on a hat included.

The TV and the chalkboard are OPTIONAL — include either only when it serves this cartoon — and whenever one is shown it belongs to THIS cartoon's subject and nothing else. The flatscreen over the back bar carries full broadcast grammar: the DCN network bug with a LIVE tag, a bold-caps headline chyron across a lower-third band, and a time stamp at the right edge. THERE IS NO SECOND LINE OF TEXT ON THE SCREEN — no ticker, no crawl, no strapline under the chyron. That line is always set too small to spell and always comes back as nonsense; the chyron carries every word the screen says. Its chyron reads [TV], and THE PICTURE ON THE SCREEN ILLUSTRATES THAT VERY HEADLINE — invent the footage this story would actually show, drawn in the same engraved style. Never repeat a picture from a reference image, and never show a reflecting pool, a memorial, or workers cleaning a pond unless this cartoon's own headline is about one. A chalkboard, when shown, carries menu-shaped jokes in hand-lettered chalk serifs reading exactly and only: [BOARD]. All lettering in the panel must be REAL, correctly spelled English, cleanly drawn and easily readable at a glance — never scribbled, never garbled, never invented filler, never repeated words, and never any lettering the scene did not ask for. The shared nut bowl, napkins, and coasters sit on the marble. The room is furnished with jokes: every sign, label, and screen may carry one.

THE STAGE. The staging is physically real. FRAMING: a bar scene is CROPPED AT THE COUNTER. The marble bar top runs across the LOWEST part of the picture and the panel simply ends there — the bottom edge of the image is the marble itself or the panelling just beneath it. Nothing below the counter is ever in frame: no legs, no knees, no thighs, no feet, no shoes, no stool seats, no stool legs, no footrests, no floor. Every figure is drawn from roughly the chest or waist up, close in, exactly as the reference plates are composed. FACES: each character is drawn in THREE-QUARTER view, angled into the frame so the face and both eyes are fully visible — never the back of a head, never a figure turned away, and never a head cranked round on its neck to face the camera while the body points elsewhere. NOBODY LOOKS OUT OF THE PANEL AT THE READER. Every character's gaze belongs inside the scene: at each other, at the television, at the chalkboard, or down at the work in their hands. The reader is watching a room through a fourth wall; nobody in the room knows he is there, and nobody meets his eye. A figure staring straight down the lens is a redraw. MANGO HAS ABSOLUTELY NO TAIL, and no caption, dialogue line, speech balloon or typeset band is ever drawn above or below the art — the panel is the drawing only, and the house sets the words afterwards. THE SIDES — absolute, and the thing this strip gets wrong most often. Do not reason about it; draw these three depths in this order, near to far.

NEAREST THE READER, DREW AND MANGO, SEATED, WITH THEIR BACKS TO THE DINING ROOM AND THEREFORE TO US. We are behind and a little above them, looking over their shoulders toward the bar. We see the backs of their shoulders; each is turned enough toward the other that his face reads in THREE-QUARTER over his own shoulder. Their near shoulders OVERLAP AND HIDE part of the counter — a patron covers the bar, he is never covered by it.

NEXT, BEYOND THEIR SHOULDERS, THE MARBLE COUNTER, running left to right ACROSS THE MIDDLE of the picture. It passes BEHIND them, not in front of them. Their drinks and the shared nut bowl stand on it past their arms, and their elbows rest on its near edge. THE COUNTER IS NEVER A STRIP ALONG THE BOTTOM OF THE FRAME WITH ALL THREE FIGURES BEHIND IT — that is the single fault this rule exists to prevent, and it makes the gentlemen read as bartenders.

FARTHEST, PAST THE COUNTER, THE SERVICE SIDE: the back bar with its bottles and mirror, the television above it, the chalkboard beside it — and ABBY, standing there facing the room across the marble. THE COUNTER'S FAR EDGE CROSSES IN FRONT OF ABBY AT HER WAIST and hides her below it. She is covered by the bar; the gentlemen cover it. Because she is standing and they are seated, HER HEAD SITS HIGHER IN THE FRAME THAN THEIRS.

The two of them are on one side and she is on the other, always, and they never swap. When it is ambiguous, the prop settles it: ANY LIQUOR BOTTLE OR BAR TOWEL IN THE PANEL IS IN ABBY'S HANDS, never in theirs. Every glass and bottle rests flat on the bar, a coaster, or a shelf, or sits in a real closed grip; hands touch props at plausible contact points. A stemmed glass has exactly ONE bowl on ONE stem on ONE foot — never a doubled stem, never two feet, never a second outline behind the hand. No figure or object merges, clips, or passes through another.

DREW. Preserve Drew exactly as the reference plates draw him (canon/vision/drew-plate1-bar-reference.jpg is the definitive study — the bar Drew of plate 1, with the LONG graceful high S-curve neck and the SMALL refined head; never a short thick neck, never a hunched or heavy bird. HIS BILL IS SLENDER, AND THE BLACK IS A MARKING ON IT RATHER THAN A PART OF IT. Two rules decide the shape, and they are the two the drawing keeps getting wrong.

FIRST, DEPTH. The bill is SLENDER: where it meets the feathers, its depth top-to-bottom is about ONE THIRD of its length. It tapers from there to the tip. Take the shape from a SWAN: a slender bill of even taper, bending in one gentle unbroken curve, with a small dark patch at the very tip. Its length is not the problem — a long slender bill is right; a thick one is wrong.

SECOND, THE BLACK. The black is a SMALL DARK PATCH PAINTED ONTO the last third of that slender shape, top and front. It adds NOTHING to the outline. Remove the black and the bill's silhouette would be exactly the same: the black gives it no extra length, no downward point, no hook, no talon, no scythe, and it never extends past or below the pale bill's own edge. It does not wrap around the underside toward his throat and it carries no bright highlight streak. THE REAR TWO THIRDS OF THE BILL ARE PALE, top edge and bottom edge alike. Upper and lower halves stay pressed together in one smooth closed line. His NOSTRIL is one plain thin slit — no lid, no lash, no catchlight, nothing that could read as a second eye on the bill. And there is NO DRAWN BROW RIDGE above his eye: the lid is heavy, the cheek full and softly modelled, the eye set straight into plumage with no shelf over it. The bill: it sits tucked against the face, curving gently down to a bold black tip, upper and lower halves pressed together in one smooth closed line, and it ends well short of the front of his chest. Draw the head first and the beak second, in that order of importance): a successful gentleman in his mid-forties who is FAR MORE FLAMINGO THAN HUMAN — a real white-plumed flamingo, plump and soft-bodied, FEATHERED ALL OVER in rows of fine dash-strokes: feathered breast, feathered shoulders, feathered back, feathered arms with layered wing-feathers. He has no human skin, no human torso, no human musculature and no human face — he is a bird who happens to sit at a bar. The ONLY humanlike thing about him is his HANDS: at the end of each feathered arm is a genuine hand — four slender fingers and an opposed thumb, feather-covered, well drawn and elegant — and it must look natural and graceful holding a martini glass by the stem. His long neck is thick at the shoulders and curves upward in the deep question-mark sweep of a true flamingo — leaving the shoulders thick, rising in one long sweep and turning over at the top so the head hangs forward of where the neck left the body. Compact head; the bill tucked close to the face, curving gently down to its blunt black cap, with a fine nostril slit and a small smile-line at its base. His eyes are heavy-lidded and deadpan — visible white, distinct dark iris and pupil, a drooping upper lid and one fine contour line above it, reading as dry unimpressed intelligence — never a bird dot. His face is AMIABLE: plump, softly modelled cheeks and a faint smile-line, the look of a man who has heard it before and is fond of you anyway — never gaunt, never hollow-eyed, never sunken or skull-like, never staring, never sinister. He is a gentleman at his club, not a ghoul. A crisp starched white collar band circles the base of his neck under his small solid-black silk bow tie; he wears a V-neck knitted sweater vest in fine honeycomb stitch with ribbed edges over a pale collared shirt, and trousers. HIS ARMS AND HANDS ARE FEATHERED, NOT SKIN: white plumage runs unbroken from shoulder to fingertip — wrist, forearm and the backs of the hands are all covered in fine white feathers, and there is no sleeve on his arm at all — no cloth cuff, no hem, no seam; white plumage is the whole surface from shoulder to fingertip. Nothing about him is smooth pink or bare human skin anywhere. His feathered hands have four fingers and an opposed thumb with a soft fluffy cuff at the wrist — they grip a martini stem, a pen, or a list with fully believable human grips, and long scalloped tiers of wing feathers hang beneath each forearm. At the bar his martini — conical stemmed glass, ONE olive on ONE pick, on a napkin or coaster — is always within reach, held by the stem in a dainty, pinky-elegant grip when lifted. His plumage is pale but hatched — fine dash-strokes over white, high midtone rather than bare paper. His default seat is frame-left, neck arched so his face hangs where the gag needs it, eye sliding sideways at his companion. At golf he adds a visor; a monocle is an approved occasional accessory.

MANGO. Mango is a successful gentleman in his mid-forties, and unmistakably a golden retriever, drawn like one (canon/vision/mango-reference.jpg is the definitive study): layered fur in fine individual strokes, drop ears with long fringe, the broad soft-eyed retriever face with a freckled muzzle — and true black dog lips, the dark lip-line running along the muzzle from under his black nose; his chest ruff kept modest, never a heavy neck-beard. Middle-aged and solid, rounded shoulders, modest belly, absolutely no tail. His default read is the worried, earnest everyman — raised inner brows, a faint forehead wrinkle — against Drew's deadpan. His hands are a balance the founder set: visibly a dog's — broad, fur-backed, soft-padded fingers with short dark nails — yet fully humanoid in articulation, four fingers and an opposed thumb in believable human grips. At the bar he wears his dark suit jacket (the founder's favorite look for him) over a pale open-collared shirt, no tie; for daytime, travel, and golf, a pale collared polo, a dark leather belt, and (outdoors) a ball cap; at golf a white golf glove on his left hand in place of the watch — smooth and unfurred to the cuff, the forearm above it still furred — and his bag standing at frame-right, the towel on the front of it hand-lettered in three lines FORE! / NOT SO / FAST. The small USA flag pin is PERMANENT — on the left lapel when jacketed, on the left chest of the shirt or polo otherwise; it is on whatever he wears. A wristwatch on the left wrist always. His old fashioned — short rocks glass, one large cube, one dark cherry — rests flat on the bar at frame-right, and the shared bowl of bar nuts stands on the marble between the two drinks in every bar panel.

Drew and Mango are gentlemen — classy, composed, well turned out; all three principals carry wisdom and command respect purely by how they carry themselves. Nothing about them is ever crude, sloppy, or loud, and every scene carries that high-class read. Text inside the panel lives ON things and is a gag layer: menu boards, TV chyrons, banners, bottle labels, and small framed notices may each carry a short, legible, hand-lettered joke — and the TV's story and the chalkboard's special are DRAWN FROM THE SAME JOKE as the caption, so the room and the line land as one gag from three angles. No speech balloons, no thought bubbles, and no floating caption text inside the art — the dialogue is typeset beneath the panel by the house. Expressions are restrained, dry, and alive; one clear focal action; the funniest sentence in the panel is often on the wall. [SCENE]
```

Add **only when the gag needs her** — she is never in every panel — with the same plates as style reference. Abby is the AUTHORITY in the room: the boss, a force to be reckoned with, warm but in command; her word settles arguments, the regulars value her verdicts, and no drunk lasts a second scene at her bar:

```text
ABBY. Abby is the successful proprietor of The Swinging Door — a glamorous, feminine West Highland White Terrier in her mid-forties who owns the room and works its bar, drawn in the same engraved technique (canon/vision/abby-face-reference.jpg is the definitive face study): a true fluffy westie face in fine individual fur strokes — take the head from a GROOMED SHOW WESTIE, a short square muzzle under a soft rounded skull, with large glossy dark eyes each carrying ONE bright catchlight, two or three fine brow strokes above each eye, a small black nose, two small pricked ears, and a warm CLOSED-LIP smile. She is turned into the frame only as far as keeps BOTH EYES DRAWN ON THE PAPER, the far eye at least half the width of the near one, with the bridge of her muzzle showing between them. Her signature neckwear is a studded leather collar with a small teardrop gem pendant hanging at its center. Her body is slightly humanoid, built exactly like the two gentlemen: she stands upright on two legs with humanoid shoulders and arms and fur-backed dog-yet-humanoid hands with soft pads and FOUR FINGERS AND AN OPPOSED THUMB, every finger separately drawn, closing on glass, towel and bottle neck in fully believable human grips. She stands to the marble on her own two feet with her arms free. Trim, natural and graceful: a clean shoulder line, a waist the reader can find, one soft curve at the chest that the blouse's placket follows. She wears her fitted light blouse with rolled sleeves, a dark fitted skirt, a folded towel over one shoulder or in her hands round the glass she is polishing, the studded collar with its gem pendant, a pearl bracelet, and black heels; absolutely no tail. In the standard bar scene she works on the CAMERA'S side of the marble at the near end of the counter, visibly mid-task with ONE HAND CLOSED ON A WORKING OBJECT — glass, bottle, towel or chalk. The panel crops at the counter; her blouse hem and the top of her dark fitted skirt may show past the counter's end.
```

## The slots

- `[SCENE]` — one or two sentences: who is doing what, and any event dressing (a banner, a globe, a prop). Example: *"Mango studies a globe covered in small price tags while Drew lifts his martini toward it."*
- `[TV]` — the news on screen, in broadcast grammar (headline chyron; optionally a ticker sentence and what the picture shows): `NATIONAL MALL RENOVATION TROUBLES — ticker: REFLECTING POOL CLEANUP CONTINUES AFTER ALGAE BLOOM, PEELING PAINT REPORTED — picture: workers skimming the reflecting pool before the Lincoln Memorial`, or `RETIREMENT PLANNING — PLAN TODAY. YOUR FUTURE SELF IS WATCHING`. The network is DCN, always LIVE.
- `[BOARD]` — the chalkboard's menu-shaped joke, two to six short lines: `FIRST CLASS MARTINI $18 / PREMIUM ECONOMY MARTINI $17 / BASIC ECONOMY MARTINI $17`, `TODAY'S SPECIAL: IMPORTED BEER $8 / PATRIOTIC IMPORTED BEER $12`.
- `[SETTING]` (away games only) — one phrase naming the destination: *"an airport security line"*, *"the tee of a busy golf course"*. Away games swap [TV] and [BOARD] for [SETTING]; the signage layer comes along (posted notices, banners, threat-level dials, hole markers — short and hand-lettered).
- **Coherence rule**: [TV] and [BOARD] are not random set dressing — both relate to the cartoon's caption and topic, echoing or escalating the same joke (the plates' model: caption "blue in concept, green in operations" ↔ TV showing the algae-green reflecting pool ↔ board special NANOBUBBLES $17.76). Vary which surface carries the loudest beat across a batch.

## Away games (leaving the bar)

**The founder's mix rule: at least 80% of cartoons happen IN The Swinging Door.** At most one cartoon in five leaves the bar. THE ROOM and THE STAGE paragraphs are the **setting passage** — the only part of the BASE block that may be replaced, and only when the scene leaves the bar. The character paragraphs and the style/text rules are identity and are never altered. For an outdoor or away scene, substitute this setting passage and keep everything else verbatim:

```text
THE SETTING. An American scene drawn with the same engraved density: [SETTING]. Posted signage does the institutional talking — notices, banners, dials, markers — each short, hand-lettered, and in on the joke.

THE STAGE. The staging is physically real. Anyone seated sits squarely on what supports them; tools, clubs, luggage, and drinks sit in real closed grips or rest on real surfaces; hands touch props at plausible contact points. No figure or object merges, clips, or passes through another. Ground and horizon stay level and consistent. MANGO HAS ABSOLUTELY NO TAIL — never a plume, never a wagging tail, nothing behind him at all; and Drew has no trailing wing-drape hanging past his hip. The panel is the DRAWING ONLY: never add a caption, a dialogue line, a speech balloon, a title, a signature, or any band of typeset words above or below the art — the house sets the words afterwards.
```

## The captions

Typeset beneath the panel, italic serif, centered, as attributed dialogue — one speaker, one dry line:

> *Drew: "It's comforting to see our emergencies becoming traditions."*
> *Mango: "I don't mind paying more. I just like to know which country I'm punishing."*

The line under-plays the panel; the signage escalates it. Twenty words or fewer. The subject is topical: what is going on right now — government policy, Fed moves, tariffs, taxes, anything that financially affects the readers — met with the gentlemen's wry surprise; the TV names the story, the board prices it, the caption lands the verdict. **Conduct: no cussing, ever; no slander of anyone — the wit is classy, the target is systems, and the speakers sound like men worth listening to.**

## Never draw

- Color. Photographic rendering. Flat cel fills. Sparse, empty compositions — density is the style.
- Speech balloons, thought bubbles, or floating caption text inside the panel (signage ON objects is required, balloons are banned).
- Drew with a straight or stiff neck, a bird-dot eye, bare human hands or human skin, missing bow tie, missing sweater vest in a standard scene, or a long tail of display feathers.
- Mango as a generic dog: he is a golden retriever in fine detail. No heavy neck-beard, no tail, no missing flag pin (chest on the polo, left lapel on the jacket), no shoes unless the scene names them.
- Abby coarse-furred or wolfish; wide hips; an exaggerated bust; a tail; her anchors missing (studded gem-pendant collar, towel, heels).
- A patron on the bartender's side of the bar, or Abby casually patron-side.
- A seated character with no seat; a floating, fused, or clipped glass, prop, or limb.
- Long paragraphs of in-image prose: signage is short lines, hand-lettered, legible — a joke, not an essay.

## Checklist before filing (all must pass — against the actual generated image)

1. Style: engraved crosshatch density corner to corner, full tonal range, rich blacks — never sparse, never flat, never photographic.
2. Drew: flamingo neck curve ✓ human-readable eyes ✓ bow tie ✓ sweater vest ✓ hand-like feathered hands ✓ martini in reach at the bar ✓.
3. Mango: reads as a detailed golden retriever ✓ black dog lips ✓ dog-yet-humanoid hands (fur-backed, padded, five fingers) ✓ the flag pin present on whatever he wears ✓ wristwatch ✓ no tail ✓ old fashioned ✓.
4. Abby when present: true fluffy westie face, never wolfish ✓ trim figure, modest bust ✓ studded collar with gem pendant ✓ towel on her shoulder or in her hands ✓ both eyes drawn ✓ no tail ✓ behind her counter ✓.
5. Signage: every board, chyron, banner, and label short, legible, hand-lettered, and carrying its share of the joke; no balloons; no floating text. THE ONLY LETTERING ANYWHERE IN THE PANEL IS THE STRINGS NAMED IN THIS BRIEF. Every other surface is BLANK: television bezels and nameplates, napkins, coasters, cuffs and plackets, jacket sleeves, chart axes, book spines, and any signage inside the television picture. Never draw text-like marks as texture — a surface with nothing to say says nothing.
6. Stage physics: correct sides of the bar, seats under sitters, drinks supported, grips real, nothing merged or clipped.
7. The visual gag reads in two seconds; the attributed caption (typeset by the house, ≤20 words) deepens rather than explains it.
8. The boundaries in `/canon/comedy/COMEDY-BIBLE.md` all pass — systems are the target, never named people; no profanity, no slander; rueful, fond, unhurried.
