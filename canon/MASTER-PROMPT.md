# The Master Prompt

<!-- BRAND: "The Swinging Door" is the series/bar name throughout this document -->

Purpose: the one page an AI reads before drawing. The BASE block below is assembled
from the whole canon — now governed by `canon/HARRINGTON-VISION.md` and the plates
in `canon/vision/` — and is pasted **verbatim** into every image request; only the
slots change per cartoon (away games additionally swap the setting passage — see
below). Do not paraphrase the base — paraphrase is how the strip drifts. When two
documents disagree, the Harrington vision wins; below it, the authority ladder in
`canon/README.md` decides; never average.

## How to use

1. Paste the BASE block exactly as written (the fenced block only — nothing outside the fence).
2. Fill the slots: `[SCENE]`, `[TV]`, `[BOARD]`. Slot language obeys each character's vocabulary: Mango and Abby have **hands**; Drew has **feathered hands** (hand-shaped, feather-covered — never bare human skin).
3. Give the image model the reference plates for the style and every character in the scene: the four Harrington plates in `canon/vision/` are the standing style-and-character reference. Over the studio connector, fetch them with `get_model_sheet`; working directly in the repo, attach the files.
4. The caption is **never rendered by the image model**. The house typesets it beneath the finished art as attributed italic dialogue — `Drew: "…"` / `Mango: "…"` / `Abby: "…"` — via `file_cartoon` over the connector or `npm run dialogue` in the repo. In-image signage, by contrast, IS the image model's job (see the text rules inside the BASE block).
5. **Look at every generated image before filing it.** Run the checklist below against the actual pixels. Any failure is a redraw with the fault named in the prompt — never a filing.

## BASE block

```text

Confident contour lines over the hatching. The panel is composed full, corner to corner, and rewards a second look. The footage itself carries NO lettering anywhere — not on a sign, a shopfront, a licence plate, a road sign, a carton, a label, a receipt, a chart axis, a seat back, a screen or a vehicle; where the scene would carry writing, DRAW THAT SURFACE BLANK. All lettering is REAL, correctly spelled English, cleanly drawn, easily readable at a glance — never scribbled, garbled, invented filler, repeated words, or lettering the scene did not ask for. THERE IS A SIZE BELOW WHICH NOTHING IS LETTERED AT ALL: if a word would be drawn smaller than the height of a character's eye, IT IS NOT DRAWN — that surface is completely blank, plain and unmarked. It governs every small object: club faces, watch dials and screens, zip pulls, nameplates, bottle caps, buttons, pen barrels, clock numerals, and every secondary line on a printed form. Marks that merely LOOK like writing come back as broken glyphs: a blank club face is correct, a scribbled one a redraw. Letter only the two or three surfaces this cartoon's joke actually needs, large enough to read; leave everything else clean. A figure staring straight down the lens is a redraw. MANGO HAS ABSOLUTELY NO TAIL. No caption, dialogue line, speech balloon or typeset band is ever drawn above or below the art: the panel is the drawing only, the house sets words afterwards. NOBODY IS WEARING THEIR OWN SKIN: feathers and fur GROW OUT OF them, never drawn as a garment. NO cuff, NO hem, NO seam, NO shoulder line, NO lapel edge, NO closing line anywhere in plumage or coat. DREW'S ARM ENDS IN A HAND. Long wing feathers belong on his BACK and SIDES, end at his hip, never hang past it, never run down the arm to become the hand. On Mango's coat and Abby's clothes fur meets cloth at the garment's own edge, nowhere else.

THE HOUSE NAME IS BUILT INTO THE OBJECT, NEVER ONTO THE CAST. It is made of the object's own material and takes the object's own light, curve and wear — a mark you could feel with a thumb, following the surface as the surface bends, sitting where a maker would sign, in the house's engraved script, small and neat and sized to the object. THE TEST: if removing it would leave the object looking untouched then it was printed ON and it is wrong. Never a sticker, decal, patch, tag, badge or floating label lying on top. COUNT IT BEFORE YOU FINISH: find the ONE object in this panel that most obviously belongs to the cast or the house — the golf bag, the towel, the bill folder, the tote, the tray — and put the mark on THAT ONE first, before any other lettering in the picture, even when it stands in the background and even when it already carries a joke of its own, the two marks sitting apart. A panel that HAS such an object and leaves it unmarked is unfinished. A panel with NO cast-owned object in it needs no mark, and none is invented for it — never add a prop to a scene just to carry the name.

THE BAR IS ONE LEVEL. COUNT THE HORIZONTAL MARBLE SURFACES IN THIS PANEL: THERE IS EXACTLY ONE. A single continuous flat slab of pale grey veined MARBLE at one height across the whole picture, moulded walnut edge below it, and EVERY drink, bowl, napkin, coaster and prop stands on THAT ONE SURFACE. A second marble surface at another height anywhere is a redraw, and a small marble table with the real bar behind it is the commonest form of it. The back bar's shelving is WALNUT joinery, not a counter.

THE SEATING IS THE SAME EVERY DAY: studded oxblood leather club chairs with brass nailheads along their backs. In the standard bar shot only the TOPS OF THEIR BACKS show along the very bottom of the frame, behind the gentlemen's shoulders — never the seats, never the legs, never a footrest, never the floor.

THE BAR HEIGHT IS FIXED, AND NOBODY MERGES INTO IT. The marble's top edge crosses each seated gentleman just below mid-chest, the same height in every cartoon, so the whole chest, both shoulders and both arms stay above it. There is a clean drawn edge where the counter passes him and his forearms rest ON TOP of the marble with the hands visible; no shoulder, chest or sleeve dissolves into the counter.

THE SIDES — absolute, and the thing this strip gets wrong most often. Draw three depths, near to far, and MEASURE THEM AGAINST THE FRAME, because 'nearer' and 'farther' are opinions and fractions are not. Working UP from the bottom edge: the LOWEST 45% belongs to DREW AND MANGO, close and large; the MARBLE COUNTER crosses between 40% and 60%, passing BEHIND their shoulders so their shoulders overlap and hide parts of it; ABBY, WHEN SHE IS NAMED IN THIS BRIEF, stands BEYOND that counter with its far edge crossing her at the waist near 60% and her head high in the TOP THIRD; and the BACK BAR, the television and the chalkboard fill the space above and behind her. Marble drawn below 30% of the frame is a redraw — that is the exact mistake that lines all three up on one plane and turns two patrons into bartenders. AND WHEN ABBY IS NOT NAMED, THE SPACE BEHIND THE COUNTER IS EMPTY. COUNT THE FIGURES BEFORE YOU DRAW ANY OF THEM: this panel contains the characters this brief names and NOBODY ELSE. If the brief does not name Abby there is NO ONE on the service side — no bartender, no server, no barback, no second dog, no figure in a waistcoat polishing a glass. It is empty walkway with the back bar behind it, and it stays empty. Inventing someone to stand there is a redraw.

NEAREST THE READER, DREW AND MANGO, SEATED, EACH TURNED A QUARTER OUTWARD FROM THE BAR. We are behind and a little above them, looking over their shoulders toward it. THE TURN IS IN THE SHOULDERS AND CHEST, NOT IN THE NECK: each man's body sits at about FORTY-FIVE DEGREES to the marble rather than square to it, so his outer shoulder is nearest us and his chest is already coming round before his head does. He is neither flat-backed to us nor square to us — he sits cornerwise. Drew is frame-LEFT turned to his RIGHT toward Mango; Mango is frame-RIGHT turned to his LEFT toward Drew. Because the BODY has done the turning the head only follows it, and BOTH EYES LAND ON THE PAPER with no crank in the neck: the far eye is at least HALF THE WIDTH of the near one, the bridge of the muzzle or bill showing between them. Drew's bill therefore crosses the picture at an ANGLE, coming toward us as well as across, never lying flat and side-on. Their near shoulders still OVERLAP AND HIDE part of the counter — a patron covers the bar, he is never covered by it.

NEXT, BEYOND THEIR SHOULDERS, THE MARBLE COUNTER, running left to right ACROSS THE MIDDLE of the picture. It passes BEHIND them, not in front. Their drinks and the shared nut bowl stand on it past their arms and their elbows rest on its near edge. THE COUNTER IS NEVER A STRIP ALONG THE BOTTOM OF THE FRAME WITH ALL THREE FIGURES BEHIND IT — that is the single fault this rule exists to prevent, and it makes the gentlemen read as bartenders.

FARTHEST, PAST THE COUNTER, THE SERVICE SIDE: the back bar with its bottles and mirror, the television above it, the chalkboard beside it — and ABBY when she is named, facing the room across the marble. THE COUNTER'S FAR EDGE CROSSES IN FRONT OF HER AT THE WAIST and hides her below it: she is covered by the bar, the gentlemen cover it. She stands and they sit, so HER HEAD SITS HIGHER IN THE FRAME THAN THEIRS.

The two of them are on one side and she is on the other, always, and they never swap. When it is ambiguous the prop settles it: ANY LIQUOR BOTTLE OR BAR TOWEL IN THE PANEL IS IN ABBY'S HANDS, never in theirs. A stemmed glass has exactly ONE bowl on ONE stem on ONE foot — never a doubled stem, never two feet, never a second outline behind the hand.

Take the shape from a SWAN: a slender bill of even taper, bending in one gentle unbroken curve, with a small dark patch at the very tip. Its length is not the problem — a long slender bill is right; a thick one is wrong.

The rear two thirds are PALE; along the OUTER THIRD ONLY, the black IS the outline — it continues the bill's own curve rather than swelling into a bulb — with one bright highlight ribbon inside it. His eyes are heavy-lidded and deadpan — visible white, distinct dark iris and pupil, a drooping upper lid and one fine contour line above it — never a bird dot. ## The slots

- `[SCENE]` — one or two sentences: who is doing what, and any event dressing (a banner, a globe, a prop). Example: *"Mango studies a globe covered in small price tags while Drew lifts his martini toward it."*
- `[TV]` — the news on screen, in broadcast grammar (headline chyron; optionally a ticker sentence and what the picture shows): `NATIONAL MALL RENOVATION TROUBLES — ticker: REFLECTING POOL CLEANUP CONTINUES AFTER ALGAE BLOOM, PEELING PAINT REPORTED — picture: workers skimming the reflecting pool before the Lincoln Memorial`, or `RETIREMENT PLANNING — PLAN TODAY. YOUR FUTURE SELF IS WATCHING`. The network is CNBC, always LIVE.
- `[BOARD]` — the chalkboard's menu-shaped joke, two to six short lines: `FIRST CLASS MARTINI $18 / PREMIUM ECONOMY MARTINI $17 / BASIC ECONOMY MARTINI $17`, `TODAY'S SPECIAL: IMPORTED BEER $8 / PATRIOTIC IMPORTED BEER $12`.
- `[SETTING]` (away games only) — one phrase naming the destination: *"an airport security line"*, *"the tee of a busy golf course"*. Away games swap [TV] and [BOARD] for [SETTING]; the signage layer comes along (posted notices, banners, threat-level dials, hole markers — short and hand-lettered).
- **Coherence rule**: [TV] and [BOARD] are not random set dressing — both relate to the cartoon's caption and topic, echoing or escalating the same joke (the plates' model: caption "blue in concept, green in operations" ↔ TV showing the algae-green reflecting pool ↔ board special NANOBUBBLES $17.76). Vary which surface carries the loudest beat across a batch.

## Away games (leaving the bar)

**The founder's mix rule: at least 80% of cartoons happen IN The Swinging Door.** At most one cartoon in five leaves the bar. THE ROOM and THE STAGE paragraphs are the **setting passage** — the only part of the BASE block that may be replaced, and only when the scene leaves the bar. The character paragraphs and the style/text rules are identity and are never altered. For an outdoor or away scene, substitute this setting passage and keep everything else verbatim:

## The captions

Typeset beneath the panel, italic serif, centered, as attributed dialogue — one speaker, one dry line:

> *Drew: "It's comforting to see our emergencies becoming traditions."*
> *Mango: "I don't mind paying more. I just like to know which country I'm punishing."*

The line under-plays the panel; the signage escalates it. Twenty words or fewer. The subject is topical: what is going on right now — government policy, Fed moves, tariffs, taxes, anything that financially affects the readers — met with the gentlemen's wry surprise; the TV names the story, the board prices it, the caption lands the verdict. **Conduct: no cussing, ever; no slander of anyone — the wit is classy, the target is systems, and the speakers sound like men worth listening to.**

## Never draw

- Color. Photographic rendering. Flat cel fills. Sparse, empty compositions — density is the style.
- Speech balloons, thought bubbles, or floating caption text inside the panel (signage ON objects is required, balloons are banned).
- Drew with a straight or stiff neck, a bird-dot eye, bare human hands or human skin, missing bow tie, missing sweater vest in a standard scene, or a long tail of display feathers.
- Mango as a generic dog: he is a golden retriever in fine detail. No heavy neck-beard, no tail, no missing flag pin (chest on the polo, left lapel on the jacket), no shoes unless the scene names them.
- Abby coarse-furred or wolfish; wide hips; an exaggerated bust; a tail; her anchors missing (studded gem-pendant collar, towel, heels).
- A patron on the bartender's side of the bar, or Abby casually patron-side.
- A seated character with no seat; a floating, fused, or clipped glass, prop, or limb.
- Long paragraphs of in-image prose: signage is short lines, hand-lettered, legible — a joke, not an essay.
