# The Master Prompt

<!-- BRAND: "The Swinging Door" is the series/bar name throughout this document -->

Purpose: the one page an AI reads before drawing. The BASE block below is assembled
from the whole canon — now governed by `canon/HARRINGTON-VISION.md` and the plates
in `canon/vision/` — and is pasted **verbatim** into every image request; only the
slots change per cartoon (away games additionally swap the setting passage — see
below). Do not paraphrase the base — paraphrase is how the strip drifts. When two
documents disagree, the Harrington vision wins; below it, the authority ladder in
`canon/README.md` decides; never average.

## How to use

1. Paste the BASE block exactly as written (the fenced block only — nothing outside the fence).
2. Fill the slots: `[SCENE]`, `[TV]`, `[BOARD]`. Slot language obeys each character's vocabulary: Barclay and Abby have **hands**; Drew has **feathered hands** (hand-shaped, feather-covered — never bare human skin).
3. Give the image model the reference plates for the style and every character in the scene: the four Harrington plates in `canon/vision/` are the standing style-and-character reference. Over the studio connector, fetch them with `get_model_sheet`; working directly in the repo, attach the files.
4. The caption is **never rendered by the image model**. The house typesets it beneath the finished art as attributed italic dialogue — `Drew: "…"` / `Barclay: "…"` / `Abby: "…"` — via `file_cartoon` over the connector or `npm run dialogue` in the repo. In-image signage, by contrast, IS the image model's job (see the text rules inside the BASE block).
5. **Look at every generated image before filing it.** Run the checklist below against the actual pixels. Any failure is a redraw with the fault named in the prompt — never a filing.

## The slots

- `[SCENE]` — one or two sentences: who is doing what, and any event dressing (a banner, a globe, a prop). Example: *"Barclay studies a globe covered in small price tags while Drew lifts his martini toward it."*
- `[TV]` — the headline chyron on screen, in broadcast grammar, ONE line (the screen never carries a ticker or a second line of text): `RETIREMENT PLANNING — PLAN TODAY. YOUR FUTURE SELF IS WATCHING`. The network is CNBC, always LIVE. An empty slot draws the set switched off, dark glass.
- `[BOARD]` — the chalkboard's menu-shaped joke, two to six short lines: `FIRST CLASS MARTINI $18 / PREMIUM ECONOMY MARTINI $17 / BASIC ECONOMY MARTINI $17`, `TODAY'S SPECIAL: IMPORTED BEER $8 / PATRIOTIC IMPORTED BEER $12`.
- `[SETTING]` (away games only) — one phrase naming the destination: *"an airport security line"*, *"the tee of a busy golf course"*. Away games swap [TV] and [BOARD] for [SETTING]; the signage layer comes along (posted notices, banners, threat-level dials, hole markers — short and hand-lettered).
- **Coherence rule**: [TV] and [BOARD] are not random set dressing — both relate to the cartoon's caption and topic, echoing or escalating the same joke (the plates' model: caption "blue in concept, green in operations" ↔ TV showing the algae-green reflecting pool ↔ board special NANOBUBBLES $17.76). Vary which surface carries the loudest beat across a batch.

## Away games (leaving the bar)

**The founder's mix rule: at least 80% of cartoons happen IN The Swinging Door.** At most one cartoon in five leaves the bar. The bar's staging paragraphs — the `BAR_ONLY_PARAGRAPHS` set in `lib/generate.ts`: THE ROOM, the screen-and-board paragraph, THE STAGE, the one-level/seating/height rules and the SIDES cluster — are the **setting passage**, the only part of the BASE block that may be replaced, and only when the scene leaves the bar. The character paragraphs and the style/text rules are identity and are never altered. For an outdoor or away scene, substitute this setting passage and keep everything else verbatim:

```text
THE SETTING. An American scene drawn with the same engraved density: [SETTING]. Posted signage does the institutional talking — notices, banners, dials, markers — each short, hand-lettered, and in on the joke. THERE IS A SIZE BELOW WHICH NOTHING IS LETTERED: a word that would be drawn smaller than a character's eye is NOT drawn — that surface stays blank. A printed prop carries at most four short lines, held UP in a hand, never lying on the ground or a surface; and nothing is tagged — no price tag, swing tag, sticker or ticket hangs from anything.

THE STAGE. The staging is physically real. Anyone seated sits squarely on what supports them; tools, clubs, luggage, and drinks sit in real closed grips or rest on real surfaces; hands touch props at plausible contact points. No figure or object merges, clips, or passes through another. Ground and horizon stay level and consistent. FRAMING: an away scene is CROPPED AT THE BELT, exactly as a bar scene is cropped at the counter. The bottom edge of the picture cuts every figure at the waistband and the panel simply ends there. NOTHING BELOW THE BELT IS EVER IN FRAME: no hips, no thighs, no knees, no legs, no trousers below the waistband, no ankles, no feet, no shoes, and no ground underfoot. Every figure is drawn from roughly the chest to the belt, close in and large, filling the frame with the place reading clearly behind them — never a wide landscape with small whole figures standing in it. A leg that tapers away and ends without a foot is the proof this rule was ignored: the fix is not to draw the foot, it is to end the panel higher. FACES: each character is drawn in THREE-QUARTER view, angled into the frame so the face and both eyes are fully visible, Drew frame-left and Barclay frame-right, each turned toward the other rather than toward us. ## The captions
```

Typeset beneath the panel, italic serif, centered, as attributed dialogue — one speaker, one dry line:

> *Drew: "It's comforting to see our emergencies becoming traditions."*
> *Barclay: "I don't mind paying more. I just like to know which country I'm punishing."*

The line under-plays the panel; the signage escalates it. Twenty words or fewer. The subject is topical: what is going on right now — government policy, Fed moves, tariffs, taxes, anything that financially affects the readers — met with the gentlemen's wry surprise; the TV names the story, the board prices it, the caption lands the verdict. **Conduct: no cussing, ever; no slander of anyone — the wit is classy, the target is systems, and the speakers sound like men worth listening to. The chalkboard is held to the same standard: it prices the joke — it NEVER urges drinking hard, fast or often.**

## Never draw

- Color. Photographic rendering. Flat cel fills. Sparse, empty compositions — density is the style.
- Speech balloons, thought bubbles, or floating caption text inside the panel (signage ON objects is required, balloons are banned).
- Drew with a straight or stiff neck, a bird-dot eye, bare human hands or human skin, missing bow tie, missing sweater vest in a standard scene, or a long tail of display feathers.
- Barclay as a generic dog: he is a golden retriever in fine detail. No heavy neck-beard, no tail, no missing flag pin (chest on the polo, left lapel on the jacket), no shoes unless the scene names them.
- Abby coarse-furred or wolfish; wide hips; an exaggerated bust; a tail; her anchors missing (studded gem-pendant collar, towel, heels).
- A patron on the bartender's side of the bar, or Abby casually patron-side.
- A seated figure with nothing behind him (the top of his club chair back shows at the frame's edge); a floating, fused, or clipped glass, prop, or limb.
- Long paragraphs of in-image prose: signage is short lines, hand-lettered, legible — a joke, not an essay.
