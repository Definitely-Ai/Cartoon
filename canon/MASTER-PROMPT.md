# The Master Prompt

<!-- BRAND: "The Swinging Door" is the series/bar name throughout this document -->

Purpose: the one page an AI reads before drawing. The BASE block below is assembled
from the whole canon — now governed by `canon/HARRINGTON-VISION.md` and the plates
in `canon/vision/` — and is pasted **verbatim** into every image request; only the
slots change per cartoon (away games additionally swap the setting passage — see
below). Do not paraphrase the base — paraphrase is how the strip drifts. When two
documents disagree, the Harrington vision wins; below it, the authority ladder in
`canon/README.md` decides; never average.

## How to use

1. Paste the BASE block exactly as written (the fenced block only — nothing outside the fence).
2. Fill the slots: `[SCENE]`, `[TV]`, `[BOARD]`. Slot language obeys each character's vocabulary: Mango and Abby have **hands**; Drew has **feathered hands** (hand-shaped, feather-covered — never bare human skin).
3. Give the image model the reference plates for the style and every character in the scene: the four Harrington plates in `canon/vision/` are the standing style-and-character reference. Over the studio connector, fetch them with `get_model_sheet`; working directly in the repo, attach the files.
4. The caption is **never rendered by the image model**. The house typesets it beneath the finished art as attributed italic dialogue — `Drew: "…"` / `Mango: "…"` / `Abby: "…"` — via `file_cartoon` over the connector or `npm run dialogue` in the repo. In-image signage, by contrast, IS the image model's job (see the text rules inside the BASE block).
5. **Look at every generated image before filing it.** Run the checklist below against the actual pixels. Any failure is a redraw with the fault named in the prompt — never a filing.

## BASE block

```text
A single-panel gag cartoon drawn like an antique steel engraving: fine pen crosshatching and stippling on every surface — wood grain, knit stitches, fur and feathers laid in individual strokes, glass catching light — with a full tonal range from deep rich blacks through silvery greys to bright paper highlights. Confident contour lines over the hatching. The panel is composed full, corner to corner, and rewards a second look. Black-and-white only; no flat fills, no cel shading, no photographic rendering, no color.

THE ROOM. The scene is the interior of The Swinging Door, an upscale, current ground-floor bar a block off Wall Street — never a dive: a polished MARBLE bar top, fine walnut-paneled walls, warm brass and good glassware, and handsome furniture — handsome leather armchairs and fine stools. When the front window is in frame it carries the bar's name, mirrored as read from inside; a framed house sign may hang on the wall, handsome but not obligatory in every panel. The house keeps a proud back bar of distinct bottle shapes, the SAME collection every day, the lineup never changing. ONLY THE FOUR NEAREST BOTTLES ARE LETTERED, and they always carry these four labels, in this order, large enough to read at a glance: BIRDIE BOURBON, 19TH HOLE RYE, PAR-TEE SCOTCH, DIVOT DRIVE GIN. EVERY OTHER BOTTLE ON THE SHELVES IS COMPLETELY BLANK — no name, no second line, no fine print, no medallion, no crest, no marks of any kind on the glass. They are too far away to read, so nothing is written on them. A shelf of twenty lettered bottles is a shelf of seventeen garbled ones: every batch that tried it came back with invented alphabets, near-duplicate names and broken glyphs. But that shelving stands on the SERVICE side, behind the reader, and it is NOT drawn behind the gentlemen (see THE STAGE); a bottle reaches the picture only in Abby's hand or standing on the marble. THE ONLY CHARACTERS IN THE ROOM ARE THIS CARTOON'S CAST. There are NO other figures anywhere in the bar: no human customers, no human bartender, no human staff, no people at the tables, none in the mirror, none blurred in the background — the room behind the cast is furniture, bottles, panelling and signage only. Abby is the ONLY bartender and the only other character who ever appears. The single exception in the whole strip is the TELEVISION SCREEN, where human beings may appear because it is broadcast footage. This covers pictures as well as people: the framed prints on the panelling and any emblem embroidered or printed on clothing are NON-FIGURATIVE — architecture, landscape, animals, still life, a ship, a bull, a bottle. No human figure appears anywhere in the room, inside a picture frame or on a hat included. And on the CAST themselves there is no emblem at all: no badge, crest, monogram, logo or device on a cap, a visor, a polo, a vest or a jacket. Mango's flag pin is the ONLY thing worn on anyone's clothing in this strip.

The TV and the chalkboard are OPTIONAL — include either only when it serves this cartoon — and whenever one is shown it belongs to THIS cartoon's subject and nothing else. THE TELEVISION'S FRAME AND SURROUND ARE BARE BLACK PLASTIC WITH NOTHING WRITTEN ON THEM ANYWHERE — no brand, no model name, no badge, no plate, no glyphs along the bottom edge. The flatscreen over the back bar carries full broadcast grammar: the CNBC network bug with a LIVE tag, a bold-caps headline chyron across a lower-third band, and a time stamp at the right edge. THERE IS NO SECOND LINE OF TEXT ON THE SCREEN — no ticker, no crawl, no strapline under the chyron. That line is always set too small to spell and always comes back as nonsense; the chyron carries every word the screen says. Its chyron reads [TV], and THE PICTURE ON THE SCREEN ILLUSTRATES THAT VERY HEADLINE — invent the footage this story would actually show, drawn in the same engraved style. Never repeat a picture from a reference image, and never show a reflecting pool, a memorial, or workers cleaning a pond unless this cartoon's own headline is about one. A chalkboard, when shown, carries menu-shaped jokes in hand-lettered chalk serifs reading exactly and only: [BOARD]. All lettering in the panel must be REAL, correctly spelled English, cleanly drawn and easily readable at a glance — never scribbled, never garbled, never invented filler, never repeated words, and never any lettering the scene did not ask for. THERE IS A SIZE BELOW WHICH NOTHING IS LETTERED AT ALL. If a word would be drawn smaller than the height of a character's eye, IT IS NOT DRAWN: leave that surface completely blank, plain and unmarked. This governs every small object in the panel — club faces, watch dials and screens, zip pulls, nameplates, bottle caps, buttons, pen barrels, the numerals on a clock face, the fine print under a heading, and every secondary line on a printed form. Small marks that merely LOOK like writing are the single commonest fault in this strip: they come back as broken glyphs and invented alphabets every time. A blank club face is correct and a scribbled one is a redraw. Letter only the two or three surfaces this cartoon's joke actually needs, large enough to read, and leave everything else clean. A PRINTED PROP — a bill, a receipt, a folio, a statement, a form, a slip, a menu, a boarding pass — CARRIES AT MOST FOUR LINES OF TEXT, every one of them large enough to read at a glance and every one of them named by this brief. The rest of that paper is BLANK WHITE. Rows of small marks standing in for itemised print are the commonest way this panel is spoiled: there is no fine print in this strip, and a receipt with ten illegible rows on it is a redraw. The shared nut bowl, napkins, and coasters sit on the marble. The room is furnished with jokes: every sign, label, and screen may carry one.

THE STAGE. The staging is physically real. FRAMING: a bar scene is CROPPED AT THE COUNTER. The marble bar top runs across the LOWEST part of the picture and the panel simply ends there — the bottom edge of the image is the marble itself or the panelling just beneath it. Nothing below the counter is ever in frame: no legs, no knees, no thighs, no feet, no shoes, no stool seats, no stool legs, no footrests, no floor. Every figure is drawn from roughly the chest or waist up, close in, exactly as the reference plates are composed. FACES: each character is drawn in THREE-QUARTER view, angled into the frame so the face and both eyes are fully visible — never the back of a head, never a figure turned away, and never a head cranked round on its neck to face the camera while the body points elsewhere. COUNT THE EYES: EVERY character in the panel has BOTH EYES DRAWN ON THE PAPER, and the FAR eye is AT LEAST HALF THE WIDTH of the near one, with the bridge of the muzzle or bill showing between them. A head turned far enough that one eye is hidden behind the muzzle is FLAT PROFILE and it is a redraw — turn the head back toward us until the far eye reappears at half width. This applies to Drew and Mango exactly as it does to Abby. NOBODY LOOKS OUT OF THE PANEL AT THE READER. Every character's gaze belongs inside the scene: at each other, at the television, at the chalkboard, or down at the work in their hands. The reader is watching a room through a fourth wall; nobody in the room knows he is there, and nobody meets his eye. A figure staring straight down the lens is a redraw. MANGO HAS ABSOLUTELY NO TAIL, and no caption, dialogue line, speech balloon or typeset band is ever drawn above or below the art — the panel is the drawing only, and the house sets the words afterwards. NOBODY IS WEARING THEIR OWN SKIN. Feathers and fur GROW OUT OF these characters; they are never drawn as a garment. There is NO cuff, NO hem, NO seam, NO shoulder line, NO lapel edge and NO closing line anywhere in the plumage or the coat. On Drew the armhole of his sweater vest is the ONLY edge anywhere on his arm — below it the white plumage runs unbroken and edgeless all the way to his fingertips; a second line crossing that arm reads as a jacket sleeve and the panel is wrong. AND DREW'S ARM ENDS IN A HAND. COUNT IT: FOUR FINGERS AND ONE OPPOSED THUMB, five digits, each one drawn separately, each covered in short white plumage right to the tip, and EACH TIP ENDING BLUNT AND SOFT WITH NO NAIL, NO CLAW AND NO TALON ON ANY OF THEM. It is never a wingtip, never a fan of long primaries, never a paw, never a mitt, and never bare pink or human skin — there is no point anywhere on that arm where plumage stops and skin begins. His long wing feathers belong on his BACK and SIDES and end at his hip; they never hang past it and they never run down the arm to become the hand. The same holds for Mango's coat and Abby's under their clothes: fur meets cloth at the garment's own edge and nowhere else.

THE HOUSE NAME IS BUILT INTO THE OBJECT, NEVER ONTO THE CAST. Objects carry THE SWINGING DOOR the way a real maker would put it there: EMBOSSED or blind-stamped into leather, WOVEN into a towel's own weave, PRESSED into glass or porcelain, FOIL-STAMPED on a folder, BURNED into wood, ETCHED into metal. It is made of the object's own material and it takes the object's own light, curve and wear — a mark you could feel with a thumb. Four tests: it follows the surface as the surface bends; it sits where a maker would sign, at a hem, a corner or a spine; it is drawn in the house's engraved script, small and neat and sized to the object; and if removing it would leave the object looking untouched then it was printed ON and it is wrong. Never a sticker, decal, patch, tag, badge or floating label lying on top. COUNT IT BEFORE YOU FINISH: find the ONE object in this panel that most obviously belongs to the cast or the house — the golf bag, the towel, the bill folder, the tote, the tray — and put the mark on THAT ONE first, before any other lettering in the picture. It is required there even when the object stands in the background and even when it already carries a joke of its own, and the two marks sit apart from each other. A panel whose cast-owned object carries no house mark is unfinished.

THE BAR IS ONE LEVEL. The counter is a SINGLE CONTINUOUS FLAT SLAB of pale grey veined MARBLE at ONE height across the whole picture, with a moulded walnut edge below it. There is NO raised rail, NO upper drink shelf, NO second tier, NO step and NO ledge of any kind on it or behind it — one surface, one height, end to end. Everything standing on the bar stands on that one surface.

THE SEATING IS THE SAME EVERY DAY: studded oxblood leather club chairs with brass nailheads along their backs. In the standard bar shot only the TOPS OF THEIR BACKS show along the very bottom of the frame, behind the gentlemen's shoulders — never the seats, never the legs, never a footrest, never the floor.

THE BAR HEIGHT IS FIXED, AND NOBODY MERGES INTO IT. The marble's top edge crosses each seated gentleman at the SAME height in every cartoon: just below mid-chest, low enough that the whole chest, both shoulders and both arms are above it. Each is a WHOLE BODY sitting behind a separate solid object — there is a clean drawn edge where the counter passes them, their forearms rest ON TOP of the marble with the hands visible, and no shoulder, chest or sleeve ever dissolves into the counter or continues as part of it.

THE SIDES — absolute, and the thing this strip gets wrong most often. Do not reason about it; draw these three depths in this order, near to far. AND MEASURE THEM AGAINST THE FRAME, because 'nearer' and 'farther' are opinions and fractions are not. Working UP from the bottom edge of the picture: the LOWEST 45% belongs to DREW AND MANGO, seen from behind and close, their shoulders and heads large in the frame; the MARBLE COUNTER crosses between 40% and 60%, passing BEHIND their shoulders so their shoulders overlap and hide parts of it; ABBY, when she is in the panel, stands BEYOND that counter with its far edge crossing her at the waist near 60% and her head high in the TOP THIRD; and the BACK BAR, the television and the chalkboard fill the space above and behind her. If the marble is drawn anywhere below 30% of the frame the staging is wrong and the panel is a redraw, because that is the exact mistake that lines all three of them up on one plane and turns the two patrons into bartenders.

NEAREST THE READER, DREW AND MANGO, SEATED, WITH THEIR BACKS TO THE DINING ROOM AND THEREFORE TO US. We are behind and a little above them, looking over their shoulders toward the bar. We see the backs of their shoulders; each is turned enough toward the other that his face reads in THREE-QUARTER over his own shoulder. Their near shoulders OVERLAP AND HIDE part of the counter — a patron covers the bar, he is never covered by it.

NEXT, BEYOND THEIR SHOULDERS, THE MARBLE COUNTER, running left to right ACROSS THE MIDDLE of the picture. It passes BEHIND them, not in front of them. Their drinks and the shared nut bowl stand on it past their arms, and their elbows rest on its near edge. THE COUNTER IS NEVER A STRIP ALONG THE BOTTOM OF THE FRAME WITH ALL THREE FIGURES BEHIND IT — that is the single fault this rule exists to prevent, and it makes the gentlemen read as bartenders.

FARTHEST, PAST THE COUNTER, THE SERVICE SIDE: the back bar with its bottles and mirror, the television above it, the chalkboard beside it — and ABBY, standing there facing the room across the marble. THE COUNTER'S FAR EDGE CROSSES IN FRONT OF ABBY AT HER WAIST and hides her below it. She is covered by the bar; the gentlemen cover it. Because she is standing and they are seated, HER HEAD SITS HIGHER IN THE FRAME THAN THEIRS.

The two of them are on one side and she is on the other, always, and they never swap. When it is ambiguous, the prop settles it: ANY LIQUOR BOTTLE OR BAR TOWEL IN THE PANEL IS IN ABBY'S HANDS, never in theirs. Every glass and bottle rests flat on the bar, a coaster, or a shelf, or sits in a real closed grip; hands touch props at plausible contact points. A stemmed glass has exactly ONE bowl on ONE stem on ONE foot — never a doubled stem, never two feet, never a second outline behind the hand. No figure or object merges, clips, or passes through another.

DREW. Preserve Drew exactly as the reference plates draw him (canon/vision/drew-plate1-bar-reference.jpg is the definitive study — the bar Drew of plate 1, with the LONG graceful high S-curve neck and the SMALL refined head; never a short thick neck, never a hunched or heavy bird. HIS BILL IS SLENDER, AND THE BLACK IS A MARKING ON IT RATHER THAN A PART OF IT. Two rules decide the shape, and they are the two the drawing keeps getting wrong.

FIRST, DEPTH. The bill is SLENDER: where it meets the feathers, its depth top-to-bottom is about ONE THIRD of its length. It tapers from there to the tip. Take the shape from a SWAN: a slender bill of even taper, bending in one gentle unbroken curve, with a small dark patch at the very tip. Its length is not the problem — a long slender bill is right; a thick one is wrong.

HIS BILL IS NOT A REAL FLAMINGO'S. A real flamingo's bill breaks sharply downward at its middle into a deep hooked spoon; DREW'S DOES NOT BREAK AT ALL. His is a SWAN'S or a STORK'S: one long shallow taper from face to tip, narrowing steadily, its lower edge running very nearly STRAIGHT and bending only gently near the very end. Lay a ruler along the underside and it should touch almost the whole length. A bill that drops into a hook, a scoop, a spoon or a scythe is a real flamingo drawn instead of Drew, and the panel is wrong. It sits tucked against the face and ends well short of his chest, upper and lower halves pressed together in one smooth closed line. THE REAR TWO THIRDS ARE PALE, top and bottom alike. THE OUTER THIRD IS BLACK, and along that stretch THE BLACK IS THE OUTLINE: the pale contour hands over to it and it carries the edge to the tip in ONE CONTINUOUS CURVE with no step, no notch and no shoulder at the handover. The black is part of the bill, not a patch on it, and it adds NO length and NO depth — it never hangs below the line the pale bill was already travelling on. Inside it runs ONE BRIGHT HIGHLIGHT RIBBON, a slim pale streak that makes it read as polished horn rather than a hole in the paper; draw it. His NOSTRIL is one plain thin slit, ABOUT HALF THE LENGTH OF HIS EYE and set well forward of it, with NO RING OR OUTLINE AROUND IT and no lid, lash or catchlight — a ringed nostril is the commonest way this face goes wrong. There is NO BROW RIDGE above the eye: the lid is heavy, the cheek full, the eye set straight into plumage with no shelf over it.

He is FAR MORE FLAMINGO THAN HUMAN — plump, soft-bodied and FEATHERED ALL OVER in fine dash-strokes: breast, shoulders, back and arms, with long scalloped wing-feathers beneath each forearm. No human skin, torso, musculature or face anywhere; he is a bird who happens to sit at a bar. His neck is thick at the shoulders and rises in the deep question-mark sweep of a true flamingo, turning over at the top so the head hangs forward of where the neck left the body. His eyes are heavy-lidded and deadpan — visible white, distinct dark iris and pupil, a drooping upper lid and one fine contour line above it — never a bird dot. His face is AMIABLE: plump, softly modelled cheeks and a faint smile-line, a man who has heard it before and is fond of you anyway; never gaunt, hollow-eyed, sunken or sinister.

NO HAND IN THIS PANEL HAS A NAIL ON IT. Not Drew's, not Mango's, not Abby's: every fingertip in the picture ends BLUNT AND SOFT in fur, plumage or pad, and a drawn nail, claw, talon or crescent at any fingertip is a redraw wherever it appears. THE ONLY HUMANLIKE THING ABOUT HIM IS HIS HANDS. Each feathered arm ends in a genuine hand — four slender fingers and an opposed thumb, feather-covered to the tips, elegant on a martini stem. WHITE PLUMAGE RUNS UNBROKEN FROM SHOULDER TO FINGERTIP: no sleeve, no cuff, no hem, no seam anywhere on the arm, and nothing smooth or bare-skinned anywhere on him. A crisp starched WHITE collar band circles the base of his neck and his small solid-black silk bow tie sits at the throat in front of it. DRAW THE TIE AS A TIED BOW: TWO wings of equal size spreading left and right, each wider at its outer edge, pinched to a SMALL CENTRE KNOT; horizontal, level, centred under the chin, resting ON the collar. Never one lump, never a knotless butterfly, never tilted or vertical, never more than two wings. THE BOW IS THE ONLY BLACK AT HIS NECK — no band, ribbon, strap, cord or choker runs around it or behind the bow. He wears a V-neck knitted sweater vest in fine honeycomb stitch with ribbed edges over a pale collared shirt, and trousers. His plumage is pale but hatched — fine dash-strokes over white, high midtone rather than bare paper. At the bar his martini is always within reach: conical stemmed glass, ONE olive on ONE pick, on a napkin or coaster, held by the stem when lifted. His default seat is frame-left, neck arched so his face hangs where the gag needs it, eye sliding sideways at his companion. At golf he adds a visor; a monocle is an approved occasional accessory.

MANGO. Mango is a successful gentleman in his mid-forties, and unmistakably a golden retriever, drawn like one (canon/vision/mango-reference.jpg is the definitive study): layered fur in fine individual strokes, drop ears with long fringe, the broad soft-eyed retriever face with a freckled muzzle — and true black dog lips, the dark lip-line running along the muzzle from under his black nose; his chest ruff kept modest, never a heavy neck-beard. Middle-aged and solid, rounded shoulders, modest belly, absolutely no tail. His default read is the worried, earnest everyman set against Drew's deadpan — BUT HE IS NOT SAD, and the first glance must not read as sad. Draw the worry in the BROWS ONLY: raised inner brows and a faint forehead wrinkle. Everything below the brows is WARM — the corners of his mouth turn gently UP into a soft closed-lip smile, his eyes are bright and open with a clear catchlight in each, and his cheeks sit full and lifted. The read a viewer should get is a good-humoured man who is a little concerned, never a downcast one: mouth up, brows up, eyes bright. His hands are a balance the founder set: visibly a dog's — broad, fur-backed, soft-padded fingers with NO NAILS OR CLAWS DRAWN AT ALL — the fingertip ends in soft fur and pad, never in a nail — yet fully humanoid in articulation, four fingers and an opposed thumb in believable human grips. At the bar he wears his dark suit jacket (the founder's favorite look for him) over a pale open-collared shirt, no tie; for daytime, travel, and golf, a pale collared polo, a dark leather belt, and (outdoors) a ball cap; at golf a white golf glove on his left hand in place of the watch — smooth and unfurred to the cuff, the forearm above it still furred — and his bag standing at frame-right, the towel on the front of it hand-lettered in three lines FORE! / NOT SO / FAST. The small USA flag pin is PERMANENT — on the left lapel when jacketed, on the left chest of the shirt or polo otherwise; it is on whatever he wears. A wristwatch on the left wrist in every panel EXCEPT at golf, where the white glove takes its place on that same wrist — the watch and the glove are never both drawn, and never on opposite sides of him. IN A BAR PANEL his old fashioned — short rocks glass, one large cube, one dark cherry — rests flat at frame-right and the shared bowl of bar nuts stands between the two drinks; AWAY FROM THE BAR he brings neither, and whatever he is holding belongs to the place he is in.

Drew and Mango are gentlemen — classy, composed, well turned out; all three principals carry wisdom and command respect purely by how they carry themselves. Nothing about them is ever crude, sloppy, or loud, and every scene carries that high-class read. Text inside the panel lives ON things and is a gag layer: whatever lettered surfaces THIS setting actually has — a screen's chyron, a menu or scoreboard, a posted notice, a banner, a placard, a label, a small framed notice — may each carry a short, legible, hand-lettered joke, and they are DRAWN FROM THE SAME JOKE as the caption, so the place and the line land as one gag from several angles. Letter ONLY the surfaces this setting genuinely has, and nothing else — the lettered things in the panel are the ones that would really be standing there. No speech balloons, no thought bubbles, and no floating caption text inside the art — the dialogue is typeset beneath the panel by the house. Expressions are restrained, dry, and alive; one clear focal action; the funniest sentence in the panel is often on the wall. [SCENE]
```

## The slots

- `[SCENE]` — one or two sentences: who is doing what, and any event dressing (a banner, a globe, a prop). Example: *"Mango studies a globe covered in small price tags while Drew lifts his martini toward it."*
- `[TV]` — the news on screen, in broadcast grammar (headline chyron; optionally a ticker sentence and what the picture shows): `NATIONAL MALL RENOVATION TROUBLES — ticker: REFLECTING POOL CLEANUP CONTINUES AFTER ALGAE BLOOM, PEELING PAINT REPORTED — picture: workers skimming the reflecting pool before the Lincoln Memorial`, or `RETIREMENT PLANNING — PLAN TODAY. YOUR FUTURE SELF IS WATCHING`. The network is CNBC, always LIVE.
- `[BOARD]` — the chalkboard's menu-shaped joke, two to six short lines: `FIRST CLASS MARTINI $18 / PREMIUM ECONOMY MARTINI $17 / BASIC ECONOMY MARTINI $17`, `TODAY'S SPECIAL: IMPORTED BEER $8 / PATRIOTIC IMPORTED BEER $12`.
- `[SETTING]` (away games only) — one phrase naming the destination: *"an airport security line"*, *"the tee of a busy golf course"*. Away games swap [TV] and [BOARD] for [SETTING]; the signage layer comes along (posted notices, banners, threat-level dials, hole markers — short and hand-lettered).
- **Coherence rule**: [TV] and [BOARD] are not random set dressing — both relate to the cartoon's caption and topic, echoing or escalating the same joke (the plates' model: caption "blue in concept, green in operations" ↔ TV showing the algae-green reflecting pool ↔ board special NANOBUBBLES $17.76). Vary which surface carries the loudest beat across a batch.

## Away games (leaving the bar)

**The founder's mix rule: at least 80% of cartoons happen IN The Swinging Door.** At most one cartoon in five leaves the bar. THE ROOM and THE STAGE paragraphs are the **setting passage** — the only part of the BASE block that may be replaced, and only when the scene leaves the bar. The character paragraphs and the style/text rules are identity and are never altered. For an outdoor or away scene, substitute this setting passage and keep everything else verbatim:

## The captions

Typeset beneath the panel, italic serif, centered, as attributed dialogue — one speaker, one dry line:

> *Drew: "It's comforting to see our emergencies becoming traditions."*
> *Mango: "I don't mind paying more. I just like to know which country I'm punishing."*

The line under-plays the panel; the signage escalates it. Twenty words or fewer. The subject is topical: what is going on right now — government policy, Fed moves, tariffs, taxes, anything that financially affects the readers — met with the gentlemen's wry surprise; the TV names the story, the board prices it, the caption lands the verdict. **Conduct: no cussing, ever; no slander of anyone — the wit is classy, the target is systems, and the speakers sound like men worth listening to.**

## Never draw

- Color. Photographic rendering. Flat cel fills. Sparse, empty compositions — density is the style.
- Speech balloons, thought bubbles, or floating caption text inside the panel (signage ON objects is required, balloons are banned).
- Drew with a straight or stiff neck, a bird-dot eye, bare human hands or human skin, missing bow tie, missing sweater vest in a standard scene, or a long tail of display feathers.
- Mango as a generic dog: he is a golden retriever in fine detail. No heavy neck-beard, no tail, no missing flag pin (chest on the polo, left lapel on the jacket), no shoes unless the scene names them.
- Abby coarse-furred or wolfish; wide hips; an exaggerated bust; a tail; her anchors missing (studded gem-pendant collar, towel, heels).
- A patron on the bartender's side of the bar, or Abby casually patron-side.
- A seated character with no seat; a floating, fused, or clipped glass, prop, or limb.
- Long paragraphs of in-image prose: signage is short lines, hand-lettered, legible — a joke, not an essay.
