# Inspecting a panel before anyone sees it

Nobody looks at a cartoon until it has been through this. Not the founder, not
a review page, not a chat message saying "here's how it came out".

## Why this document exists

Twice in one night a panel was declared correct after an inspection that only
looked at what had just been changed.

The first time, twelve panels were verified as having the right staging using a
test that could not tell a patron from a bartender. The founder needed two
seconds: *"they look like they are behind the bar with abby."*

The second time, a panel was sent over with the words "both fixes verified"
after its bill and its golf bag were examined closely and nothing else was
examined at all. Both characters' trouser legs ended in rounded stumps with no
feet, planted in the turf like fence posts. The founder needed two seconds
again: *"look at the feet or lack of feet or legs in that."*

Both failures have the same shape, and it is not carelessness. It is
**checking your own work instead of checking the picture.** Attention goes to
the delta — the thing just fixed, the thing just argued about — and the other
ninety per cent of the panel becomes background. The delta is the one region
where a fault is *least* likely, because it is the region someone was just
thinking about.

> **Inspect the panel, not the change.**

## The method

**1. Look at it whole, once.** First impression, no checklist. Write down
anything that feels wrong before you start looking for specific things — a
first impression is the only unprimed look you get, and it is the closest thing
to the founder's eye.

**2. Then crop and enlarge.** A 1024×1536 panel shown at thumbnail size hides
everything that matters. Missing feet, a ringed nostril, a garbled word, a
sixth finger, a strap around a bow tie — all invisible small, all obvious at
100%. Every panel gets its regions cut out and looked at again:

| Region | What hides there |
| --- | --- |
| **The bottom edge** | Legs ending in stumps, feet, floor that should not be in frame, cropped-off hands |
| **Each head** | The bill's black and its nostril, eyes built wrong, a gaze aimed at the reader, brow ridges |
| **Each pair of hands** | Nails, claws, wingtips, missing fingers, a grip closing on nothing |
| **Each arm and shoulder** | A seam, cuff or hem that turns plumage into a sleeve |
| **Every piece of lettering** | Garbled or doubled words, invented filler, misspellings |
| **Every prop** | A missing house mark, a mark printed on rather than made in, a prop that belongs to another setting |

**3. Sweep region by region, not down a list.** Top edge to bottom edge, left
to right, across the whole picture. The checklist is a floor. The faults that
cost the most are the ones no rule anticipated, and a list-driven inspection is
structurally blind to them — you cannot check a box nobody has written yet.

**4. Say what is wrong before saying what is right.** A report that opens with
what worked has already decided the answer.

## What disqualifies an inspection

- It only covers the rules that changed recently.
- It was done at thumbnail size with no crops.
- It reports a panel as correct without naming the regions actually examined.
- It was done by the same pass that made the change, with no fresh look.

## The standing checks

Everything in `canon/MASTER-PROMPT.md`'s own checklist, plus each character
bible's *Forbidden* section. Those lists are the floor. Read them; do not
recite them from memory, because they change weekly and memory does not.

The two that have failed most often, and are worth naming here:

- **The crop.** A bar scene ends at the counter; an away scene ends at the
  belt. Nothing below it, ever — no thighs, knees, legs, feet, shoes or ground.
  A leg that tapers away and ends without a foot means the panel should have
  ended higher, not that a foot should be added.
- **Occlusion tells you which side of the bar someone is on.** A patron's
  shoulders cover the counter. A bartender is covered by it. Reasoning about
  who is "behind" the bar without checking what covers what is how twelve
  panels passed a test that could not fail.
